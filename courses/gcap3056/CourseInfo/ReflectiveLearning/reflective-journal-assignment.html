Reflective Learning Journal — Assignments

GCAP 3056 · Taking a Stand: Turning Research Insights into Policy Recommendations · Fall 2026
35% of your final grade · individually assessed · CILOs 3 & 4 · entries submitted via Moodle

In one sentence: the journal is your record of how you worked — what you did in your tabs, what you asked AI, what peers and the teacher contributed — and what changed in you because of it. The grade is not the output; it is the judgment you add between the AI draft and your final words.

1. What to write about — the rule of thumb

Every entry is built from evidence you already have. Open the course Google Doc and look at your own tab and its sub-tabs — that is your primary source:

  • What you have been doing in the tabs and sub-tabs under your name — week by week, task by task;
  • The requests you made — info requests, enquiries, emails, accessinfo.hk submissions, forum posts;
  • The documents you read — which sources you opened, cited, or rejected, and why;
  • The chat sessions you had with AI — your prompts and your verdicts on the replies (keep / revise / reject);
  • What Simon has done under his tabs — use them as a model of how to organise a working tab: what to keep, what to show, what to annotate.

New — AI agent access: more recently, direct access to an AI agent has become available to you. You are encouraged to experiment with it — give it real tasks from your project, watch what it does, and write down what worked, what failed, and what you had to fix yourself. Experiments (including failed ones) are excellent journal material.

2. The assignments — Weeks 8, 9, 10

REQUIRED Entry 3 · Week 8 — Work process and workflow

Write about how you get the job done. Using your tabs as evidence, describe your workflow over the past weeks and answer:

  1. You. What did you actually do — the decisions, the edits, the moments you overruled an AI reply?
  2. AI. What did you delegate to AI (and to the agent, if you experimented) — drafts, summaries, extraction, checking? What did it get wrong?
  3. Peers and teacher. What came from your team, from other groups, from the teacher — and how did their input change your work?
  4. The workflow. Put it together as a process (tabs → prompts → drafts → feedback → revision). What is one change you would make to it now?

Length: about 150 words (more is fine). Submit: draft in your journal tab → paste the link on the status page (must read ok) → post the final text in the Week 8 Moodle thread.

OPTIONAL — encouraged Entry 4 · Week 9 — Wow and Aha

Pick one:

  • (a) A Wow moment — something in the course that surprised or impressed you, and why it mattered to you.
  • (b) An Aha moment — a point where you saw how to apply a course idea to your own project, and what you did next.
  • (c) A failure worth keeping — an AI answer you nearly submitted, a request that stalled, a misunderstanding in your team: what happened, what you learned, what you will do differently.

OPTIONAL — encouraged Entry 5 · Week 10 — Curate and commit

  1. Curate: choose your strongest material from your Moodle replies and earlier tabs; rewrite, merge or replace earlier entries so the set reads as your learning story.
  2. Commit: finish with one paragraph — what habit or mindset from this course will still be there next year? (The gold standard: lasting change in behaviour and attitude.)

4. How to use AI (and the agent) — the rule

  • You are encouraged to use AI to draft any entry, and to experiment with the AI agent on real project tasks.
  • Every draft is reviewed and revised by you: no sentence you cannot explain, no claim you cannot check.
  • Your partnership with AI is a central theme of the journal — what you delegated, what it got wrong, what only you could add.
  • Keep your prompts and verdicts in your tab — they are the evidence your entry cites.
Draft a reflective journal entry of about 150 words from my tabs and Moodle
replies. Headings: (1) what I did, (2) what AI did, (3) what peers and the
teacher did, (4) what changed in my workflow. First person, plain English,
no invented facts, no citations. Mark anything you don't know as [ASK ME].

Then: answer every [ASK ME] · delete anything that does not sound like you · add one real detail from your tabs · check every claim.

Sample entry you can imitate (~170 words)

Workflow. I did: chose which of our three info-request options to send, and cut the AI's draft from 400 to 180 words. AI did: first draft, a table of what we had tried, spelling. Peers and teacher did: Wing flagged that our request went to the wrong department; the teacher's example letter showed the tone.

Where I added value: the AI's draft sounded like a complaint; my edit made it a request under the Access to Information regime — that judgment came from our class discussion, not from the model.

Wow / Aha. Wow: the AI produced a plausible citation that did not exist — I checked and it was invented. Aha: I can now use the same check-then-send workflow for my group's second request.

6. The routine — every week, same four steps

  1. Do the week's task and reply in the Moodle forum thread for that week.
  2. Work in your tabs (rule of thumb, §1) — requests, documents, chat sessions all live there.
  3. Draft your entry in your journal tab; paste the link on the status page — status must read ok (if error: share the doc → "Anyone with the link can view").
  4. When final, copy the entry into the Moodle forum thread — that is your submission.

8. Quick checklist

  • About 150 words or more, first person.
  • Built from my own tabs — requests made, documents read, AI chats (not from memory alone).
  • Drafted with AI if I like, but revised by me — no sentence I cannot explain.
  • Says what AI did, what peers and teacher did, and what I added.
  • Contains a Wow and/or Aha moment (Entry 4) or a lasting change (Entry 5).
  • Link pasted at the status page and status is ok.
  • Final version posted in the Moodle forum thread for that week.
